01The Big Analogy

Vehicles for crossing the same terrain A bicycle, a scout's motorbike, an armored truck, and a lawyer's opening statement are all ways of getting from "what's out there" to a destination — but you'd never use an armored truck to scout unfamiliar terrain, or a bicycle to move something that needs to survive an audit.

A systematic review is the armored truck: slow, heavily engineered, built to survive being audited line by line — every record's fate logged, nothing quietly left behind. A scoping review is the scout on a motorbike: fast, wide-ranging, mapping what terrain exists without stopping to fight every battle. A narrative/thesis review is a tour guide walking you down one curated path toward a specific viewpoint — selective on purpose, and better for it. A positioning review (the USENIX-style "Related Work" section) is a lawyer's opening statement: built to make one case, and it fails the instant it misrepresents the other side's evidence.

None of these is "the real literature review" that the others are sloppy versions of. They're different vehicles, built for different jobs. The mistake this module exists to prevent is picking the wrong one — or worse, not knowing there was a choice.

02The Two Axes That Actually Separate Them

Nineteen names is too many to hold in your head as an unordered list. Nearly all of them fall out naturally along two questions, following the distinction methodologists use between aggregative and configurative synthesis (Barnett-Page & Thomas, 2009):

  • How broad is the question? Narrow and specific ("does X change Y?") vs. broad and exploratory ("what does this field even look like?")
  • How are findings combined? Aggregative — pooling comparable findings together, assuming they measure roughly the same thing (adding) — vs. configurative — arranging diverse findings to build a new interpretation or argument (arranging)
Quick gut-check: if combining two studies' findings could sensibly produce one number (like a meta-analysis's pooled effect size), that's aggregative. If combining them produces a new sentence of interpretation instead of a number, that's configurative.

04All Nineteen Types, One Line Each

TypeOne-line definition
Systematic reviewExhaustive, protocol-driven search and synthesis to answer a focused question, with every decision logged
Meta-analysisStatistical pooling of effect sizes across studies into one combined estimate
Scoping reviewMaps the breadth of a field — what exists, what's studied, what's missing — without judging effect or quality
Mapping review / systematic mapCatalogs and codes existing research by characteristics (design, population, topic) to visualize where evidence clusters and gaps sit
Rapid reviewA systematic review's methodology compressed under a time constraint, with explicitly documented simplifications
Traditional / narrative reviewAn expert-guided, non-systematic account of a topic, organized to build understanding or argument rather than exhaustively account for all evidence
Critical reviewGoes beyond describing evidence to critique and reconceptualize it, often producing a new conceptual model
State-of-the-art reviewSurveys current/most recent understanding of a topic, prioritizing recency over historical completeness
OverviewA broad, umbrella-adjacent term for a review summarizing a body of evidence at a high level, often for a non-specialist audience
Umbrella reviewA review of reviews — treats other systematic reviews/meta-analyses as its unit of analysis
Qualitative systematic review / meta-synthesisSystematically synthesizes findings across qualitative studies into new interpretive conclusions, without pooling numbers
Meta-ethnographyA specific meta-synthesis method that "translates" concepts across qualitative studies into a higher-order interpretation
Realist reviewAsks not just "does it work" but "what works, for whom, under what circumstances, and why" — focused on underlying mechanisms
Integrative reviewCombines findings from studies of different methodologies (quantitative and qualitative) into one synthesis
Mixed studies / mixed methods reviewSystematically includes and synthesizes quantitative, qualitative, and mixed-methods primary studies side by side
Bibliometric review / citation analysisAnalyzes the literature itself as data — citation counts, co-authorship, co-citation clusters — rather than synthesizing findings
Living systematic reviewA systematic review maintained as a continuously updated, versioned artifact rather than a one-time snapshot
Systematic search and reviewUses a systematic, reproducible search, but applies non-systematic (often narrative) synthesis to the results
Systematized reviewAttempts elements of systematic-review methodology (typically as a student assignment or single-reviewer project) without meeting the full standard

05Limits / What This Module Cannot Do

This module does NOT

  • Teach you to run any of these review types — no code, no working pipeline
  • Rank them by rigor — a well-executed scoping review is not a "worse" systematic review, it answers a different question
  • Cover every review-type name ever coined — this is a representative map, not an exhaustive taxonomy

This module DOES

  • Let you correctly name what you're trying to do before you start
  • Point you to the exact module that builds that type's actual pipeline
  • Prevent the single most common beginner error: calling something a "systematic review" that isn't one

07Brain Exercise

You want to know "does cognitive behavioral therapy reduce anxiety scores more than medication?" — a single, specific, measurable comparison, and you have months and a team. Which type, and why?
Systematic review with meta-analysis. The question is narrow (one specific comparison) and the outcome is a number (anxiety score) that's plausible to pool across studies — aggregative. You have the time budget for the full protocol-driven rigor. If you only had two weeks, the same question would push you toward a rapid review instead — same shape of question, compressed methodology.

08There Are No Dumb Questions

Isn't "systematic review" just the correct name for any careful literature review?
No — it's a specific methodology with specific requirements (protocol, comprehensive search, PRISMA reporting). Calling a narrative review "systematic" because you were thorough is the single most common misuse of the term in student work.
What's actually different between a scoping review and a mapping review? They sound the same.
Close cousins. A mapping review's output is usually a coded, visual catalog of study characteristics (a literal map/chart of where evidence clusters). A scoping review's output is usually a narrative account of the field's breadth. In practice many projects do both at once — see the hub page's confused-pairs table.
Why does "systematized review" even exist as a separate category from "systematic review"?
Because a huge amount of real published work (especially student dissertations) uses systematic-review-style methods but can't meet the full standard — often just one reviewer instead of two independently screening. Naming it honestly instead of overclaiming "systematic" is the entire point of the category.
Where does the USENIX-style "Related Work" section from this course's Modules 27–28 fit on the map?
Narrow + configurative, same quadrant as critical review and realist review — it's making an argument (a comparison-driven case for novelty), not pooling numbers or mapping breadth.
Do I need to read all 19 concept pages before starting Module 1?
No. Read the ones near whatever you're trying to build. Module 1 onward builds the systematic-review engine specifically — if that's your goal, you only strictly need this page and the systematic review / meta-analysis concept pages.
