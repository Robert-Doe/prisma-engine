Module 0 — ConceptIntegrative Review

In one image A single dinner party where guests who normally never speak the same language — a statistician and a novelist — are made to sit together and actually engage, because the host believes both perspectives are needed to understand the topic fully.

An integrative review deliberately combines findings from studies of different methodologies — experimental, correlational, qualitative, theoretical — into a single synthesis, on the premise that a fuller understanding of a complex topic requires more than one kind of evidence at once. It's especially common in nursing and applied social-science research.

What makes it distinct

  • Deliberately methodologically heterogeneous inclusion criteria — this is a design choice, not a limitation being tolerated
  • Requires a synthesis method flexible enough to hold numeric and non-numeric findings side by side
  • Frequently used to synthesize theoretical/conceptual literature alongside empirical studies
Distinguishing it from mixed-methods review: the two overlap heavily in practice. Integrative review is usually the broader, looser term (can include purely theoretical works); mixed-methods review more specifically means primary studies that themselves used quantitative and qualitative methods.
How this connects

Closely related to Mixed studies / mixed methods review — see that page for the practical distinction. Both are broader and more configurative than a single-methodology Systematic review.
