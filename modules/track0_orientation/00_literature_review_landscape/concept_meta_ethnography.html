Meta-ethnography (Noblit & Hare, 1988) is the best-known specific technique for synthesizing qualitative studies. It works by systematically "translating" each study's key concepts into the terms of the others, then looking for how those translated concepts relate: do they say compatible things (reciprocal translation), contradict each other (refutational translation), or together tell a bigger story none of them told alone (a line-of-argument synthesis)?

The three translation types

  • Reciprocal — studies' concepts are directly comparable and agree
  • Refutational — studies' concepts directly conflict, and the conflict itself is worth explaining
  • Line-of-argument — no direct overlap in concepts, but combined they support a broader interpretation
Why "translation" and not "summary": the method forces you to re-express each study's finding in a shared vocabulary before comparing — which mechanically prevents the trap of listing findings study-by-study without ever combining them.
How this connects

A specific technique within the broader Meta-synthesis category — meta-synthesis is the goal, meta-ethnography is one named method for achieving it.
