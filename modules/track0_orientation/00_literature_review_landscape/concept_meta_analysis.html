Module 0 — ConceptMeta-Analysis

In one image Ten separate polls each ask the same question of different, smaller crowds. Averaged together — weighted by how many people each poll actually asked — you get one number more trustworthy than any single poll alone.

Meta-analysis is a statistical technique, not a review type on its own: it takes the effect sizes reported by multiple studies (usually the output of a systematic review's inclusion process) and pools them into one combined estimate, typically weighting larger/more precise studies more heavily, and quantifying how much the individual studies disagree with each other (heterogeneity, often reported as I²).

What it requires to be valid

  • Studies measuring a comparable outcome, expressed in a common effect-size unit
  • Enough studies to make pooling meaningful (rarely done with fewer than ~3–5)
  • Awareness that pooling doesn't fix bias in the underlying studies — garbage in, precisely-weighted garbage out
Common confusion: "systematic review" and "meta-analysis" are often said in the same breath, but a systematic review can conclude that pooling isn't appropriate (too much heterogeneity, incompatible outcome measures) and stop at a narrative synthesis instead. Not every systematic review contains a meta-analysis.
How this connects

Requires Systematic review's search and screening underneath it. Contrast with Meta-synthesis — same "combine multiple studies" goal, but for qualitative findings that can't be pooled numerically.
