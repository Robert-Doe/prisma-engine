02Commonly Confused Pairs

People say...What's actually true
"I did a thorough search, so it's a systematic review." Thoroughness alone isn't the bar — a systematic review needs a pre-registered protocol, comprehensive multi-database search, explicit eligibility criteria, and PRISMA reporting. A thorough single-reviewer search without those is a systematized review.
"Scoping review and mapping review are the same thing." Nearly, but not quite — scoping leans narrative account of breadth; mapping leans toward a coded, visual catalog of study characteristics. Many real projects are honestly a blend of both.
"Meta-analysis and meta-synthesis are the same idea for different data." Related instinct, different mechanics — meta-analysis pools numbers statistically; meta-synthesis (of which meta-ethnography is a specific method) builds a new interpretive conclusion from qualitative findings that can't be averaged.
"Umbrella review" and "overview" get used interchangeably. Both often summarize existing reviews rather than primary studies, but an umbrella review keeps systematic, researcher-facing rigor (protocol, review-quality appraisal tools) that an "overview" usually doesn't.
"Rapid review" and "rapid evidence assessment" are different things. In practice these largely overlap and are often used as synonyms; where a distinction is drawn, "rapid evidence assessment" sometimes implies an even further-compressed, policy-facing variant. Treat them as the same family, not opposites.
"A critical review is just a narrative review that's more opinionated." Close, but critical review specifically aims to reconceptualize the field — often producing a new model or typology — rather than just describing the field with a point of view.

03Scenario-Matching Quiz

Scenario A: You have 300 existing systematic reviews on different aspects of diabetes management, and you want to know where they agree, disagree, and overlap in evidence. Which type?
Umbrella review — the unit of analysis is other reviews, not primary studies.
Scenario B: A funding body wants a two-week turnaround on "what do we currently know about X" to inform a policy decision by Friday.
Rapid review — systematic-review rigor, deliberately compressed and documented as such.
Scenario C: You want to know how a technical sub-field's citation network clusters, and which three papers everyone in it cites.
Bibliometric review — the data is the citation structure itself, not the papers' findings.
Scenario D: Ten qualitative interview studies on patients' experience of chronic pain exist. You want one coherent account of what that experience is like across all of them.
Meta-synthesis (likely via meta-ethnography) — combining qualitative findings into new interpretation, not pooling numbers.
