Prerequisites: Synthesis vs. Summary

00Before You Start

Read Prerequisite 9 first if you haven't — it explains, in plain prose, the exact distinction this module turns into working code.

01The Big Analogy

Sorting laundry by owner vs. by type Three family members' laundry sits in three separate baskets, one per person. Look at Basket A and you know everything Person A owns. But you can't answer "how many blue shirts does this household have?" without digging through all three baskets and mentally combining what you find — that question was never organized for.

Now dump all three baskets onto the floor and re-sort by type: a pile of shirts, a pile of pants, a pile of socks. Same laundry, completely different question answered instantly — how many shirts, what colors, anything unusual. Nothing about the clothes changed. Only the sorting axis did.

SynthesisMatrix is the floor both piles get dumped onto. render_by_source() is the three baskets. render_by_theme() is the re-sorted piles — the moment a stack of separate summaries becomes an actual argument.

02What Happens, Step by Step

  1. You register each source (add_source()) and each theme you've identified across your reading (add_theme()) — deciding your rows and columns on purpose, not ad hoc
  2. For every source, under every theme it actually addresses, you add one short note (add_note()) — this is the row-by-row filling-in step, and it's the only place new information enters the matrix
  3. render_by_source() reads the matrix out row by row — this is what a first-draft "summary" looks like when you write it up
  4. render_by_theme() reads the exact same cells out column by column — this is what "synthesis" looks like, mechanically, from identical underlying notes
  5. coverage() counts how many sources touch each theme — the thin spots this exposes are exactly what Module 30 uses next

    lines = []

Same self.cells dictionary in both functions. The only difference is which variable is the outer loop. That's the entire mechanism — not a metaphor for the difference between summary and synthesis, the actual, literal difference.

05Limits / What You Cannot Do Yet

Can't do yet

  • Write your actual thesis prose for you — a note like "found no significant change" still needs to become a real sentence you write
  • Decide what your themes should be — that's real analytical work only you (the reader) can do, this module just organizes what you've already decided
  • Detect if two sources genuinely contradict each other on a theme — that's Module 30's job, one step further

Can do

  • Prove, with your own real notes, whether your draft is organized as summary or synthesis
  • Show you exactly where one source's findings split across multiple themes — information a source-by-source read hides
  • Surface which themes are thin (only one source addresses them) — often exactly where a thesis's own contribution belongs

07Brain Exercise

Look at the render_by_source() output above. It's not badly written — the sentences are clear. So what, specifically, makes it "just a summary" and not synthesis?
Try the test from Prerequisite 9: delete the source names and read it straight through. "Reported higher job satisfaction... found no significant change in output... found increased self-reported productivity, found decreased measured output, noted the divergence..." — you can reorder those three chunks in any order and lose nothing, because each one only relates to the source it came from, not to the others. Now do the same to render_by_theme()'s output: reordering "Self-reported outcomes" and "Objectively measured outcomes" would break the implicit argument building across them (self-report says one thing, objective measures say another — that contrast is the point). Order matters in synthesis because the paragraphs are building on each other. Order doesn't matter in summary because they aren't.

08There Are No Dumb Questions

Do I have to code this up for my actual thesis, or can I just use a spreadsheet?
A spreadsheet works completely fine for real use — rows as sources, columns as themes, exactly like this module's cells dict. This module exists to prove the mechanism is real and mechanical, not to insist you need Python for your thesis. If a spreadsheet already does this for you, you've already internalized the lesson.
What if a source doesn't fit neatly into any of my themes?
That's real, useful information, not a failure of the matrix — it might mean the source isn't as relevant as you thought, or that you're missing a theme your reading hasn't surfaced yet. Either way, a source with zero notes in the matrix is something worth deliberately deciding about, not something to force a cell for.
How many themes is "enough" for a real thesis chapter?
There's no fixed number — it depends entirely on your field and your sources. What matters mechanically is that every theme has more than one source contributing to it wherever possible; a theme with exactly one source (like "Task-type dependence" in this module's demo) is a real signal, and Module 30 uses that signal directly.
