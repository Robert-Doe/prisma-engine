Prerequisite 1 of 9Research Question Frameworks

The Big Analogy A research question usually arrives as a blurry photo: "does remote work affect productivity?" You can tell roughly what's in the picture, but you can't hand it to anyone and expect them to search for it — "productivity" could mean a hundred different things to a hundred different databases. A question framework is the darkroom process. It doesn't change what's in the photo; it develops it until the edges are sharp enough that someone else could search for the exact same thing you meant.

01PICO — for questions about an effect

Originating in evidence-based medicine (Richardson et al., 1995), PICO splits a question into four slots:

  • Population / Problem — who or what is being studied
  • Intervention — the thing being done to them
  • Comparison — what it's being measured against (a control, a placebo, an alternative)
  • Outcome — what's actually being measured

"Does remote work affect productivity?" becomes: P = knowledge workers, I = remote work arrangements, C = in-office work arrangements, O = measured productivity (output per hour, self-reported, etc.).

02PEO — for questions with no intervention

PICO assumes something is being done to a group — but a lot of real questions are observational: nobody assigned anyone to smoke, or to live near a factory. PEO drops the "Intervention/Comparison" framing for Exposure instead: Population, Exposure, Outcome. It fits etiology and risk-factor questions where you're asking "what happens to people already exposed to X," not "what happens if we give people X."

03SPIDER — for qualitative questions

PICO's Intervention/Comparison slots don't fit questions about lived experience — there's no "intervention" in "how do new parents experience sleep deprivation." Cooke, Smith & Booth (2012) proposed SPIDER instead:

  • Sample — who was studied (not "population," because qualitative samples are usually small and purposive, not representative)
  • Phenomenon of Interest — the experience or behavior being examined
  • Design — how the studies gathered data (interview, focus group, ethnography)
  • Evaluation — what outcome or finding type is relevant
  • Research type — qualitative, quantitative, or mixed
Why this matters mechanically: whichever framework you pick, its slots become the inputs to your search — each slot expands into a set of synonyms, which become one clause of a boolean query (see Prerequisite 2). Picking the wrong framework for your question type produces a search string that is precise about the wrong things.
