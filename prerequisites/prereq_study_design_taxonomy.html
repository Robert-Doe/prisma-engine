Prerequisite 4 of 9Study Design Taxonomy & Evidence Hierarchy

The Big Analogy Think of study designs like witnesses in a courtroom. An eyewitness who watched the whole thing happen under controlled conditions (a randomized trial) is more reliable than someone reconstructing events from memory months later (a retrospective study) — not because the second witness is lying, but because their account is more exposed to distortion. The "evidence hierarchy" ranks designs by how exposed to distortion (confounding, bias) their conclusions are.

01The designs, roughly strongest to weakest for causal claims

DesignWhat it does
Randomized Controlled Trial (RCT)Randomly assigns subjects to intervention vs. control — randomization is what lets you attribute the difference to the intervention rather than to some pre-existing difference between groups
Cohort studyFollows a group over time, comparing those exposed to something vs. those not — no randomization, so exposed/unexposed groups may differ in other ways (confounding)
Case-control studyStarts from the outcome (people who have a condition) and looks backward for exposure differences vs. matched controls — efficient for rare outcomes, more vulnerable to recall bias
Cross-sectional studyA single snapshot in time — can show association, cannot establish which came first (exposure or outcome)
Case series / case reportDescribes what happened to one patient or a small uncontrolled group — no comparison group at all
Qualitative studyNot on this hierarchy at all — it answers a different kind of question (experience/meaning, not causal effect size) and is evaluated on its own criteria
The pyramid is a simplification. Frameworks like GRADE treat "level of evidence" as a starting point that gets upgraded or downgraded per study (a very large, precise cohort study can outrank a small, poorly-run RCT). Don't treat design type alone as a final verdict on quality — that's exactly what Module 11's checklist-based scoring is for.

02Why this matters mechanically

Study design determines what raw numbers a paper even reports (a case-control study reports odds, a cohort study can report risk), which determines which effect-size formula applies (Prerequisite 6), and it's one of the first things a risk-of-bias checklist branches on — an RCT and a cohort study get evaluated against different questions.
