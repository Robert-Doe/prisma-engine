Prerequisite 2 of 9Boolean Search & Controlled Vocabulary

The Big Analogy Think of a database as a warehouse and your search as a set of nested sieves. AND stacks two sieves on top of each other — only what falls through both lands in your bin (narrower). OR puts two sieves side by side over the same bin — anything falling through either one counts (wider). NOT is a trapdoor under one sieve that dumps its contents in the trash before they ever reach the bin.

01The operators

  • AND — both terms must be present (narrows results)
  • OR — either term is enough (widens results)
  • NOT — exclude records containing this term (narrows, but riskily — see warning)
  • "..." phrase search — match the exact phrase, not the words scattered anywhere in the record
  • * truncation — employ* matches employee, employment, employer, employed
  • (...) parentheses — control precedence, exactly like arithmetic
Why NOT is dangerous: remote work NOT office doesn't just exclude studies about offices — it excludes any study that merely mentions an office for context, even one whose main finding is exactly what you wanted. Reviewers who use NOT carelessly silently delete relevant studies. Most search protocols avoid it entirely.

02Controlled vocabulary vs. free-text

A free-text search matches whatever words the authors happened to type. If one author wrote "remote work" and another wrote "telecommuting" and a third wrote "work from home," a free-text search for just one of those terms misses the other two.

A controlled vocabulary is a database's own fixed, hand-curated thesaurus — every record gets manually tagged with standard terms from that list, regardless of what words the author actually used. PubMed's is called MeSH (Medical Subject Headings). Search MeSH: Teleworking and you catch every record tagged that way, even the one that only ever said "distributed workforce" in its abstract.

Free-textControlled vocabulary
CatchesExact words/synonyms you typedEverything tagged with that concept, regardless of wording
MissesAny synonym you didn't think to includeAnything not yet indexed (very new terms, or databases without a thesaurus)
Best practiceCombine both with OR for the same concept — this is what "sensitive" search strategies do
