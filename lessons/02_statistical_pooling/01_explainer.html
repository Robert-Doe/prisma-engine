Concept Cluster 02Statistical Pooling

Modules 16, 17, and 19 each build one piece of statistical pooling — effect sizes, combining them, and checking whether the combination is trustworthy — but the deeper ideas connecting all three (why pooling is even valid, what it assumes, and how those assumptions get violated in practice) don't belong to any single one of them.

01The Big Analogy

Averaging temperatures from different cities, honestly Averaging today's temperature across five cities only means something if you're asking "what's the temperature like across this region today" — a real, coherent question. Average temperatures across five different days in one city and you're answering a different question (typical weather over time). Average temperature readings where one thermometer was miscalibrated, and your average is confidently wrong. Statistical pooling across studies has all three of these traps: pooling assumes the studies are estimating the same real quantity (Module 17's fixed-effect assumption), heterogeneity measures whether that assumption actually holds (Module 17's I²), and publication bias is the miscalibrated-thermometer problem — some "readings" never made it into your average at all (Module 19).

03The One Real Number That Ties Them Together

This course's own five-study demo (Modules 16-18), run for real:

  Fixed-effect:   pooled OR = 1.923   95% CI [1.297, 2.851]   "significant"
  Random-effects: pooled OR = 1.866   95% CI [0.675, 5.154]   "not significant"

  I² = 84.8%  (substantial heterogeneity — genuine disagreement between
               studies, not just sampling noise)

Same five real numbers. Same real formulas. Two different practical
conclusions, purely because of which assumption about "do these studies
estimate one true effect" you're willing to make. This is not a rounding
difference — it's the entire reason the choice between fixed- and
random-effects models is a real, consequential methodological decision
in every published meta-analysis, not a technicality.

04Why This Lives Here, Not Inside One Module

Module 16 alone can't show why the model choice matters — it only produces one study's number. Module 17 alone shows the mechanism but, read in isolation, doesn't fully land why I²=84.8% is the number that explains the two models' disagreement. Module 19 alone looks like an unrelated bolt-on. Read together, they're one continuous argument: an effect size is only as trustworthy as the pooling method's assumptions, and those assumptions are only trustworthy if the underlying evidence base isn't itself biased by what never got published.
