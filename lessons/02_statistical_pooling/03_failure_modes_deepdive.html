Concept Cluster 02 — Deep Dive 2How Pooling Misleads, Even When the Arithmetic Is Correct

Every formula in Modules 16-19 can be computed correctly and the pooled conclusion can still be wrong — not from a coding bug, but from a real, well-documented statistical trap the arithmetic alone can't catch.

01The Jingle-Jangle Fallacy

Two studies claiming to measure "productivity" might be measuring genuinely different things (the jangle fallacy — different names, actually different constructs, being wrongly treated as comparable), or two studies might use different names for what's actually the same underlying construct and get excluded from a pooling that should have included them (the jingle fallacy, the mirror problem). Module 16's SMD calculator can compute a perfectly correct standardized mean difference between two studies measuring subtly different things — the formula has no way to know the two "productivity" scales aren't really comparable. That judgment has to happen before the arithmetic, during data extraction (Module 10) and evidence-table assembly (Module 14), not after.

02Garbage In, Precisely-Weighted Garbage Out

Module 11's risk-of-bias scorer exists precisely because Module 17's meta-analysis engine has no way to detect that a pooled study's own underlying data was compromised — a poorly-randomized, unblinded trial with a real design flaw gets weighted by 1/SE² exactly like a rigorous one, and if its variance happens to be small, a flawed study can dominate a pooled estimate. Pooling amplifies precision, not trustworthiness — the two are independent axes, and conflating them is one of the most common real misreadings of a meta-analysis's forest plot.

03Simpson's Paradox

A trend can appear in several separate groups and reverse when the groups are combined. This is a real, well-documented statistical phenomenon (not unique to meta-analysis, but a real risk within it): pooling across studies with different underlying population compositions can produce a combined trend that doesn't match any individual study's own trend. This is part of why Module 17's I² check matters — high heterogeneity is one of the signals that a pooled number might be hiding exactly this kind of reversal underneath it, which is why Module 17's tutorial recommends investigating why studies disagree rather than only reporting the pooled number.

04Publication Bias's Real Bite

Module 19's Egger's test looks for funnel-plot asymmetry as a statistical signal — but the underlying real-world mechanism is worth naming directly: small studies with null or unfavorable results are measurably less likely to get published, peer-reviewed, or even written up at all, across many fields with documented evidence of this pattern. A meta-analysis pooling only what got published is, structurally, pooling a non-random sample of all the evidence that was ever actually collected — which is exactly why Module 19's tutorial insists a non-significant Egger's test on too few studies isn't reassuring, only inconclusive.

The throughline across all four traps: none of them would be caught by re-checking Module 16-19's arithmetic. Every one of them requires a human judgment call upstream (constructs genuinely comparable? primary studies genuinely trustworthy? heterogeneity genuinely investigated? evidence base genuinely complete?) that no formula, however correctly implemented, can substitute for.
